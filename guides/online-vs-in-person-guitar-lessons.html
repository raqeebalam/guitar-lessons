Online vs In-Person Guitar Lessons: Cost, Results and Who Should Pick Which

By Raqeeb Alam · Updated September 2026 · 6 min read

I teach both, so this is not a sales pitch for one side. Online lessons work very well for about 80% of students, in-person is genuinely better for a specific 20%, and the price difference in the US is large enough that it should be part of the decision.

Independent online teachers based outside the US (like me, in Pakistan) can charge $25 a lesson and still earn well, which is why the quality-per-dollar is usually best in that column. The trade-off is you need to check credentials and take a trial, since there is no music school vetting for you.

What online lessons do well

  • Everything chord- and rhythm-based. Strumming, chord changes, songs, theory, ear training. With a decent camera angle the teacher sees exactly what your hands do.
  • Consistency. No drive, no parking, no "I'll skip this week". Students who take online lessons miss fewer lessons, and consistency is the single biggest predictor of progress.
  • Recording. Zoom lessons can be recorded. Watching the 3-minute clip where your teacher fixed your G chord is worth more than any written note.
  • Choice of teacher. You are not limited to whoever lives within 20 minutes. You can pick someone who teaches your genre, your language, or who is simply a better fit.

What in-person does better

  • Physically adjusting your hands. For some beginners, a teacher gently moving a thumb into place is faster than any verbal instruction. Online, this takes a bit more back and forth.
  • Very young children (under 8). Attention span on a screen is short. Ages 10+ do fine online.
  • Playing together in real time. Internet latency makes true duets over Zoom tricky. Teachers work around this (one person plays, the other listens, then swap) but it is a real limitation.
  • Guitar setup problems. If your guitar buzzes or is hard to press, a teacher in the room can diagnose it in seconds. Online, I ask you to point the camera at the strings and we usually figure it out, but it is slower.

The camera setup that makes online lessons work

Most bad online lesson experiences come from a laptop camera pointed at the student's face. Fix this and the gap with in-person mostly disappears:

  1. Frame the guitar, not your face. Sit back so the camera sees from your chin to the guitar body. The teacher needs both hands.
  2. Phone as a second camera (optional but excellent). Join the Zoom call from your phone too, propped up looking at your fretting hand. Two angles, zero cost.
  3. Turn off Zoom's noise suppression (Settings → Audio → "Original sound for musicians"). Otherwise Zoom thinks your guitar is background noise and mutes it.
  4. Wired headphones if you have them. They stop echo and let you hear the teacher's guitar clearly.

Who should choose which

Choose online if: you are an adult or teen beginner, you want to learn songs, your budget matters, your schedule is busy, or the teachers near you don't teach what you want to play.

Choose in-person if: your child is under 8, you have a physical limitation that needs hands-on guidance, you specifically want ensemble or band coaching, or cost is not a factor and you prefer being in a room.

Do both if: you live somewhere with good local teachers but want more frequent, cheaper sessions between them. Many of my students take weekly online lessons and see a local teacher once a month.

Questions to ask any online teacher before paying

  • Can I have a free or cheap trial first? (If no, walk away.)
  • What is your camera setup? (You want to see their fretboard clearly.)
  • Do you send materials after the lesson? (Chord sheets, backing tracks, a short recap.)
  • What is your cancellation policy? (24 hours is standard and fair.)
  • Can you teach the specific songs I want? (Ask for one by name.)
